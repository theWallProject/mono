Abandoning the Quadrupole with PAMAF™ 

That headline likely captured your eye but isn’t the full story.

A recent GenomeWeb interview dives deeper into the how and why SLIM + PAMAF on a QTOF can increase the speed and sensitivity of LC-MS, demonstrating a significant shift in the conversation around instrument performance: from 100’s of fragment spectra per second to 1,000’s, and from duty cycle to ion utilization efficiency.

Duty cycle is an incomplete measure of performance as it reflects acquisition time, not how many generated ions are actually detected. Even at 95 to 99% duty cycle, most ions are discarded during sequential MS/MS selection, reducing sensitivity and missing low-abundance analytes.

Integrating SLIM with a QTOF addresses this directly. Ions are accumulated in parallel and compressed from hundreds of milliseconds into ~2 ms mobility peaks, delivering a 100× signal gain. The high resolving power of SLIM eliminates the need for narrow quadrupole isolation, enabling near-complete ion utilization with >1000 Hz speeds.

First we described it, then we demonstrated it. Now, let’s revisit it.

Sometimes what looks like one peak… isn’t.

This creates a fundamental challenge for any analysis: confidence. If structurally similar compounds elute at the same time, it becomes difficult to determine whether a signal represents one molecule, several similar species, or an unrelated interference. 

That’s one of the challenges Austin Anderson and his team at Los Alamos National Laboratory are addressing as they evaluate high-resolution ion mobility with MOBIE®.

Before working with MOBIE, the lab was already familiar with the need for additional separation dimensions to analyze complex samples, often relying on approaches like GC×GC-MS or 2D LC-MS. However, MOBIE has proven to be by far the quickest and easiest way to set up a two-dimensional separation for their analyses.

By adding an additional separation dimension between LC and the mass analyzer, ion mobility can begin resolving compounds that overlap in LC space, allowing signals to be separated that previously appeared as a single peak.

For scientists working with complex or simple samples, that additional separation can reveal compounds that were previously difficult to distinguish in traditional workflows, helping them interpret their data with greater confidence and uncover molecular details that might otherwise remain hidden.

For decades, chromatography has defined how we think about separation science.

That’s why it was notable to hear HRIM from MOBILion Systems, Inc., discussed on the podcast Concentrating on Chromatography hosted by David Oliva.

In Episode 50, our CTO Daniel DeBord explains how SLIM provides a gas-phase separation step between LC and the mass analyzer. Instead of filtering ions by mass the way quadrupoles do, HRIM separates ions by size and charge.

The result: cleaner data, faster acquisitions, and separations with peak capacities comparable to long LC gradients all achieved in milliseconds.

For expert and non-expert chromatographers alike, it raises an interesting question:

How might this additional dimension of separation be used to expand analytical capability…whether by enabling deeper multidimensional separations, offsetting long chromatographic gradients, or reducing reliance on chromatography when it isn’t necessary?

The episode also covers how PAMAF™ challenges quadrupole dependency in proteomics workflows, where HRIM is already making an impact, and what next-generation LC-ion mobility-MS platforms could look like.

Clinical research workflows are under constant pressure to deliver faster results without compromising data quality.

For Tim Garrett’s lab, integrating ion mobility added an additional gas-phase separation dimension to their analytical workflow, enabling shorter analysis times while preserving the sensitivity and specificity required for complex biological samples.

This approach allows his team to explore challenging applications, including the analysis of peptides, lipids, and metabolites with greater structural insight, and in experimental formats where traditional chromatography alone can be limiting.

As these methods continue to develop, ion mobility is helping researchers rethink how assays are designed and validated for clinical research, and what may ultimately define a standard analytical workflow.
