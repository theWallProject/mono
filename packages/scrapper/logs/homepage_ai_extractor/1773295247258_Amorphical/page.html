The Mechanism of nano-amorphous mineral

Nano-amorphous minerals are characterized by a unique disordered molecular structure that makes them highly dynamic and responsive within the body. Unlike conventional crystalline minerals, nano-amorphous minerals exist in ultra-small sizes. These formless particles can break down, releasing essential ions that participate in biological processes such as bone formation, tissue regeneration, and cellular metabolism. When nano-amorphous minerals encounter even a mild acidic or inflamed microenvironment, they immediately dissolve and release carbonate ions that neutralize acidity. This rapid, localized response helps to reduce inflammation, promote healing, and reestablish physiological stability without disturbing surrounding healthy tissue.

Inspired by nature

The blue crayfish, sometimes called the sapphire crayfish or the Florida crayfish, is a
species of crayfish that lives in fresh water without readily available calcium sources.
Over millions of years of evolution, it has developed an exceptional ability to grow a
new exoskeleton in just 3 days – 7 times faster than other crustaceans that live in
mineral-rich saltwater.

How Does the Blue Crayfish Do it?

The blue crayfish has a unique ability to store the calcium and carbonate it needs for
periodic exoskeleton regrowth in a rare and highly unstable form known as
amorphous calcium carbonate (ACC), and then use it with growth rates and
efficiency unmatched elsewhere in nature. (Amorphous means not-crystalline.)
The ACC is stored in two gastroliths, or stomach stones, attached to the crayfish’s
stomach walls. When the molting process begins, those gastroliths dissolve in
stomach acid. This rapid dissolution of calcium ions allows the crayfish to build a new exoskeleton with extraordinary speed.
Amorphical successfully imitated this process, producing nano-amorphous minerals stabilized in their amorphous form, making them suitable for human use. Today, we are developing innovative medical treatments based on the traits and chemistry of ACC.
